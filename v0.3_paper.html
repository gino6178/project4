IntentFlow: Moving a Home's Arrangement into Another Room with One Transformer

Anonymous submission

MethodExperimentsReferencesAppendix

Abstract. People who like the arrangement of another home want it in their own: the same kind of furniture, arranged the same way, in a room with another size and outline and with doors and windows elsewhere. A designer doing this keeps what matters, drops what no longer fits, adds what the new room calls for, and places everything so that the groups and their relations to the walls survive. We present IntentFlow, one transformer that makes all of these decisions. The reference's furniture, carried into the target room, and the room's walls, doors and windows are tokens of a single attention trunk; from it, heads predict which pieces a designer keeps, how strongly each relation between pieces is held and which pieces a designer adds, and a rectified-flow velocity head places the furniture. The keep, relation and addition heads are supervised by pairs of real rooms furnished by two designers with similar furniture, with no hand-written relation types or rules; hard constraints are enforced afterwards by a projection in an arrangement metric, and a style token changes the furniture's appearance without changing the arrangement. On a frozen held-out test set of 300 real room pairs and 700 reshaped and rescaled targets, IntentFlow keeps the reference arrangement with a relation distance \(D_A=0.082\), against 0.184 for LEGO-Net and 0.386 for PhyScene (p<0.001 and p<0.001), leaves 0.3% of pieces outside the room (LEGO-Net 17.5%, PhyScene 15.9%), and adds pieces with a precision of 0.41 against the designers' additions.

1. Introduction

A person browsing furnished homes finds an arrangement they like and wants it in their own home — the same kind of furniture, arranged the same way. Their room has another size and outline, and its doors and windows are elsewhere. An interior designer given this job would keep the pieces that carry the design, drop the ones that no longer fit, add what the new room calls for, and place everything so that the groups hold together and stand against the right walls (Fig. 1). We study this reference-to-room arrangement transfer: given a reference scene \(S_\text{ref}\) and an empty target room \(P_t\), produce a furnished \(P_t\) that keeps how \(S_\text{ref}\) is arranged and is physically valid.

The task is hard for three reasons. First, the decisions interact: what fits depends on where things go, where things go depends on what is kept, and what a designer adds depends on both. Second, "how a room is arranged" is not a fixed list of relation types; two designers furnishing similar furniture keep some relations and change others, and which ones survive depends on the room. Third, the result must be feasible — inside the room, free of interpenetration, with doorways clear — while a model that places furniture meets such constraints only up to its position error. Existing methods address neighbouring problems: scene generators [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] sample new layouts for a room, rearrangement models [6] move a given layout toward a regular one, and layout optimisation [7, 8] scores layouts with hand-designed terms. None takes a reference arrangement and reproduces it in another room.

IntentFlow makes the decisions with one network (Fig. 2). The reference's pieces are carried into the target room by an affine transplant and become tokens of one attention trunk together with the room's wall and opening tokens. Read once at flow time \(\tau=0\), the trunk predicts which pieces a designer keeps, how strongly each relation is held and, as set completion, which pieces a designer adds; along the flow, a velocity head moves the pieces to their places. Pairs of real rooms furnished by two designers with similar furniture supervise keeping, relation strength and additions directly. A projection enforces the hard constraints in an arrangement metric that moves neighbouring pieces together, and a style token changes the furniture's appearance without touching the arrangement.

Our contributions are:

  • One transformer that keeps, weighs, adds and places (§3.2): keep, relation-weight and addition heads share the attention trunk of a rectified-flow placement model and are learned from real designer pairs, with no hand-written relation types, hierarchy or addition rules.
  • Additions as set completion (§3.3): the model proposes one piece at a time given what is already in the room and stops when the set is likely complete; on held-out pairs its additions agree with the designers' with precision 0.41.
  • A feasibility projection in an arrangement metric (§3.4): displacement plus the change of neighbour offsets, weighted by the learned relations, so that a piece pushed out of an overlap takes its group along.
  • A frozen evaluation (§5) on 700 reshaped and rescaled targets and 300 held-out real pairs, with PhyScene and LEGO-Net run once and re-scored by the same code; IntentFlow keeps the reference arrangement closer than both at a fraction of their out-of-room rate.

2. Related Work

Indoor scene synthesis. Learned generators produce furniture layouts for a given floor plan autoregressively [1, 2], by denoising diffusion [3] or conditioned on a semantic graph [4]; PhyScene [5] adds collision and reachability guidance to a diffusion sampler. PlanIT [9] plans a relation graph and instantiates it. These methods sample a plausible layout; they do not take a reference arrangement to preserve. Earlier systems optimise interior-design guidelines written as energies [7, 8].

Rearrangement. LEGO-Net [6] learns to move a messy layout toward a regular one by iterative denoising. Given the transplant of a reference as its input, it moves the pieces toward the arrangements it was trained on, not toward the reference.

Relations and sets. Scene-graph methods use a fixed vocabulary of relation types [9, 4]. IntentFlow instead gives every pair the same geometric descriptor and learns how strongly a relation is kept from real designer pairs; pairwise terms enter as additive attention biases, as in Graphormer [10]. Deciding what to add is a completion problem over a set of furniture, related to set-based networks [11, 12] and to recommendation by graph completion [13, 14]; we complete the set one piece at a time with a stopping rule.

Flows and transformers. Flow matching [15] and rectified flow [16] regress a velocity along straight paths between two distributions; an informative source lets IntentFlow start from the affine transplant rather than from noise. The network is a permutation-equivariant transformer [17] with adaptive layer-norm conditioning [18] as in DiT [19]. The projection is solved by an augmented Lagrangian [20].

3. Method

3.1. Problem formulation

A reference scene is \(S_\text{ref}=\{(c_i,s_i,p_i,\theta_i,a_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) with category \(c_i\), 3-D size \(s_i\in\mathbb R^3_{>0}\), floor position \(p_i\in\mathbb R^2\), yaw \(\theta_i\) and asset \(a_i\) (a 3D-FUTURE model [21]). The target room \(P_t\) is a simple polygon with door and window segments. Optionally a style token \(z^\star\in\mathbb S^{31}\) and a degree of change \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) are given. The output is a layout \(\{(c_i,s_i',p_i',\theta_i',a_i')\}\) in \(P_t\) in which pieces may be removed, replaced by a smaller or restyled asset of the same kind, or added.

Arrangement distance. For pieces \(u,v\), the descriptor \(\phi_{uv}\in\mathbb R^6\) holds \(v\)'s offset in \(u\)'s frame, \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) of the relative yaw, the footprint gap and the centre distance. Walls, doors and windows are nodes framed by their segment midpoint and inward normal and use the same descriptor. The per-relation distance

$$\begin{aligned}d(\phi,\phi')=\tfrac{1}{3.5}\Big[&\tfrac{1-\cos\angle(\phi_{1:2},\phi'_{1:2})}{2}+\tfrac{1-\cos(\Delta\theta-\Delta\theta')}{2}\\&+\min\!\Big(\tfrac{|\log(\phi'_6/\phi_6)|}{\log 3},1\Big)+\tfrac12\,\big[\,[\phi_5<0.05]\neq[\phi'_5<0.05]\,\big]\Big]\in[0,1]\end{aligned}\tag{1}$$

combines offset direction, relative orientation, log distance ratio and contact (gap below 5 cm). Between two layouts of corresponding pieces,

$$D(S,S')=\frac{1}{|\mathcal E|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal E}d\big(\phi_{ij},\phi'_{ij}\big),\tag{2}$$

over all ordered pairs \(\mathcal E\) of pieces present in both. We write \(D\) for the distance of an output to its reference and \(D_A\) for the same on real pairs, where \(A\) is the reference room.

3.2. IntentFlow: one transformer

Tokens. Each reference piece is carried into \(P_t\) by the affine transplant \(\mathcal T_q\): its pose mapped proportionally between the minimum rotated rectangles of the two rooms, under the quarter turn \(q\) that best overlaps the outlines. A furniture token holds the piece's category, log size, elevation and current state \(x_{\tau,i}=(p_i/L,\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\) (\(L=3\) m) with Fourier features, the distances of its sides to the walls now and in the reference, the votes of the other pieces (where each other piece's reference relation puts it, given that piece's current pose), and its reference functional relations \(\nu_i\): offset, relative yaw and gap to the nearest wall, door and window, and what is left to undo in \(P_t\). Wall segments and openings (with their clearance boxes) of \(P_t\) are tokens of the same attention. Pairwise terms — the current geometry, the reference relation of the same pair and the offset still to undo — are added to the attention logits [10]. Adaptive layer norm [18] injects the flow time, room descriptors and a pooled code \(\zeta_\text{ref}\) of the reference's room-free relations. The trunk (Fig. 3) has 8 blocks of width 256 with 8 heads and is permutation-equivariant; the answer never enters an input.

Analysis heads. Read once at \(\tau=0\) with every piece at its transplant pose,

$$\begin{gathered}\kappa_i=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(\mathrm{MLP}_\kappa(e_i)\big),\qquad \hat w_{ij}=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(\mathrm{MLP}_w[e_i,e_j]\big),\\ r=\operatorname{softplus}\big(\mathrm{MLP}_r[\bar e_F,\bar e_R,c]\big)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{64},\end{gathered}\tag{3}$$

with \(e_i\) the final token of piece \(i\), \(\bar e_F\), \(\bar e_R\) the means over furniture and room tokens and \(c\) the conditioning vector. \(\kappa_i\) is the probability that a designer keeps piece \(i\) in this room, \(\hat w_{ij}\in[0,1]\) how much of the relation \(i\to j\) a designer keeps, and \(r_c\) the expected number of pieces of category \(c\) still to add.

Supervision from real designer pairs. A real pair is a room \(A\) and a room \(B\) of the same type from another home whose category-family sets have Jaccard similarity above 0.6 and whose area ratio lies in [0.5, 2]; pieces are matched within families by footprint size [22]. Carrying \(A\)'s pieces into \(B\)'s empty room, a floor piece is labelled kept (\(y^\text{keep}_i=1\)) if matched and dropped if unmatched while \(B\) contains its family; a matched pair \((i,j)\leftrightarrow(k,l)\) carries the preservation \(y_{ij}\). Additions are learned as set completion: a row carries \(A\)'s kept pieces and a random part of what \(B\) added, and \(n_c\) counts what \(B\) added beyond it, zero once the set is complete. The loss of the analysis heads is

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal L_\text{ana}=\mathrm{BCE}\big(\kappa_i,y^\text{keep}_i\big)+\frac{1}{|\mathcal Y|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal Y}\big(\hat w_{ij}-y_{ij}\big)^2+\frac{1}{3}\sum_c\big(r_c-n_c\log r_c\big),\\ y_{ij}=1-d\big(\phi^A_{ij},\phi^B_{kl}\big),\end{gathered}\tag{4}$$

a binary cross-entropy, a squared error and a Poisson negative log-likelihood. No annotation or rule enters the labels.

Placement head. Placement is a rectified flow [16, 15] from \(x_0=\mathcal T_q(S_\text{ref},P_t)\): positions move on straight paths and yaws along the shorter arc,

$$\begin{gathered}x_\tau=\big((1-\tau)p_0+\tau p_1,\ \theta_0+\tau\,\Delta\theta\big),\\ \mathcal L_\text{FM}=\mathbb E_{\tau}\sum_{i}m_i\Big(\big\lVert v^p_{\vartheta,i}-(p_{1,i}-p_{0,i})\big\rVert^2+R_i^2\big\lVert v^\theta_{\vartheta,i}-\Delta\theta_i\,\mathbf e_\perp(\theta_{\tau,i})\big\rVert^2\Big),\end{gathered}\tag{5}$$

in metres: \(R_i\) is the piece's half diagonal, so a yaw error counts as corner displacement; \(\mathbf e_\perp(\theta)=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\); \(m_i\) masks supervised pieces. Wall-contact and wall-parallel heads are trained with binary cross-entropy (contact within 5 cm, yaw within 4° of the room axes), and a relative term (weight 1) keeps the offsets of neighbouring pieces (within 0.3 m). Sampling takes 50 Euler steps. Analysis and placement rows share every trunk parameter.

3.3. Inference: what to keep and what to add

Keeping. Pieces that cannot fit at all go first. Then pieces are removed in the order of least expected relation mass, re-read on the remaining pieces after every removal, until the footprint fits the density budget \(B(P_t)\), with \(\rho\) the area ratio of target and reference and \(q_{95}\) the 95th percentile of furniture density in the corpus:

$$\begin{gathered}i^\ast=\arg\min_{i\in K}\ \kappa_i\sum_{j\in K}\big(\hat w_{ij}+\hat w_{ji}\big)\quad\text{while}\quad\sum_{i\in K}\mathrm{fp}_i>B(P_t),\\ B(P_t)=\max\big(\mathrm{fp}_\text{ref}\min(1,\rho+0.14),\ q_{95}\,|P_t|\big),\end{gathered}\tag{6}$$

If the projected layout is still infeasible or less walkable than the reference, up to 3 further pieces may go, but only pieces with \(\kappa_i<\tfrac12\) — pieces a designer is more likely to drop than to keep. Pieces with \(\kappa_i\approx1\) act as anchors without any list of anchor categories. A free-standing piece takes as host the piece it is most strongly related to among those with higher \(\kappa\) and keeps its host's direction in the projection. If the kept set is still infeasible, smaller assets of the same 3D-FUTURE fine category are tried, then the other ambiguous quarter turns. Candidates are compared lexicographically: feasible, as walkable as the reference, fewest departures, smallest \(\hat w\)-weighted relation loss.

Adding. Additions are made one piece at a time. With \(K\) the kept pieces and \(A\) the additions so far, the add head gives the rates \(r_c(K\cup A)\), and

$$\begin{gathered}c^\ast=\arg\max_{c\notin\mathcal X}r_c(K\cup A)\\ \text{while}\quad P(\text{no further piece})=\exp\Big(-\sum_{c\notin\mathcal X}r_c(K\cup A)\Big)\le\tfrac12,\end{gathered}\tag{7}$$

where \(\mathcal X\) holds catch-all categories (misc, decoration, other), which name no kind of furniture, and categories that did not fit. The piece is placed by the flow with the rest fixed and kept if the projected layout stays feasible and walkable; at most 6 pieces are added. An added piece starts next to its most frequent partner category already in the room, at an offset drawn from corpus statistics.

3.4. Feasibility projection \(\Pi_\theta\)

\(\Pi_\theta\) returns the feasible layout closest to the placed poses \((\hat p,\hat\theta)\) in an arrangement metric:

$$\begin{aligned}&\min_{p,\theta}\ \sum_i\lVert p_i-\hat p_i\rVert^2+\big(R_i(\theta_i-\hat\theta_i)\big)^2+0.5\sum_{i\neq j}\bar w_{ij}\big\lVert(p_i-p_j)-(\hat p_i-\hat p_j)\big\rVert^2\\\text{s.t.}\ &\mathrm{pen}(i,j)\le\alpha_{ij}\ \ (i,j\ \text{overlapping in height}),\qquad \text{corners}(p_i,\theta_i)\subset P_t,\\&\mathrm{pen}(i,b)\le0\ \ (b\in\mathcal D),\qquad \theta_i\in\Theta_i\ \ (i\ \text{locked}),\qquad \mathrm{gap}(i,\ell_i)\le0.03\,\text{m}\ \ (i\in\mathcal C)\end{aligned}\tag{8}$$

The first two terms are each piece's move (angles as corner arc length); the third changes the offsets between neighbours, with \(\bar w_{ij}=\tfrac12(\hat w_{ij}+\hat w_{ji})\) for pieces within 0.3 m of each other and 0 otherwise, so that a piece pushed out of an overlap takes its group along. \(\mathrm{pen}\) is the separating-axis penetration depth of two oriented boxes; the allowance \(\alpha_{ij}=o_{c_ic_j}\min(R_i,R_j)\) is the corpus overlap rate of the two categories times the smaller half diagonal (a chair may slide under a table). \(\mathcal D\) are door clearance boxes of the door's width (at least 1 m) and 0.9 m depth. The orientation set \(\Theta_i\) is the nearest wall's direction \(+k\pi/2\), or the host's direction for a hosted piece; a piece parallel to the walls in the reference stays parallel, other pieces are locked when the wall-parallel head says so, and added pieces always take the orientation set. Pieces the wall-contact head marks (\(\mathcal C\)) end within 3 cm of their nearest wall \(\ell_i\). The problem is solved by an augmented Lagrangian [20] (20 Adam steps per outer iteration, penalty doubled when the violation does not fall below 0.25 of the previous one, stop at 1 cm).

The hard constraints are not learned. On the endpoint estimate \(\hat x_1=\mathbb E[x_1\mid x_\tau]\) a non-convex feasible set can make them infeasible even for the exact flow (the mean of a chair left and right of a doorway lies in the doorway), and on samples any position error violates constraints on whose boundary the training targets lie. The projection enforces them per sample instead.

3.5. Style token

Style means the furniture's appearance. Each catalogue asset \(k\) gets an appearance vector \(z_k\in\mathbb S^{31}\), an MLP of its room co-membership embedding (a PPMI factorisation, cf. [23]), its CLIP image features [24] and its 3D-FUTURE style, theme and material labels, trained to rank the designer's asset of a held-out piece against same-category assets by the mean appearance of the room's other pieces [25], with a supervised contrastive term on the style label [26]:

$$\mathcal L_z=-\log\frac{\exp(\langle z_{k^\ast},\bar z_{\text{room}\setminus k^\ast}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{k\in\mathrm{cat}(k^\ast)}\exp(\langle z_k,\bar z_{\text{room}\setminus k^\ast}\rangle/\gamma)}+\mu\,\mathcal L_\text{SupCon}(z;\text{style label}),\tag{9}$$

with \(\gamma=0.1\). A token \(z^\star\) is the mean appearance of a style label. Pieces are grouped into units of identical assets; a unit's candidate is the asset of the same category and the same 3D-FUTURE fine category closest to \(z^\star\) within a size tolerance (tried as 12%, 20%, 30% per dimension). Units are ordered by their gain toward \(z^\star\), \(u_1,u_2,\dots\), and a prefix is swapped whose piece count is nearest to \(\lambda N\):

$$U(\lambda)=\{u_1,\dots,u_m\},\qquad m=\arg\min_{m'}\Big|\sum_{k\le m'}|u_k|-\lambda N\Big|.\tag{10}$$

so the swapped set only grows with \(\lambda\). The swap happens before placement, so the arrangement passes through the placement and projection unchanged. If the kept set must take smaller assets (§3.3), a swapped piece takes the smaller asset within the style label, or the smaller version of its own asset if the label has none; unswapped pieces shrink within their own appearance. The density budget of Eq. 6 is computed with the assets the pieces will have, so a restyle alone never removes a piece.

4. Data and Training

Corpus. We use 3D-FRONT [27] rooms with 5–24 pieces after duplicate removal (13,729 rooms) and the 3D-FUTURE assets [21], split by house into 10,988 training, 1,402 validation and 1,339 test rooms. No model sees validation or test rooms in training.

Real designer pairs. Training rooms and their partners give 31,112 real pairs from 9,205 rooms for the keep, relation and addition heads. Two designers' arrangements of matched pieces are a median \(D=0.35\) apart, and designer \(B\) adds pieces in 87% of pairs (2.82 per pair; measured on 6,828 pairs). Placement rows use pairs whose relation change is small (\(D\le0.15\), 3,350 pairs), the matched pieces carried from \(A\) into \(B\)'s room and placed as \(B\)'s designer did.

Augmentation. Real pairs rarely cover strongly reshaped or rescaled rooms. For 2,990 training rooms, target rooms of other outlines and sizes (L 705, pentagon 666, T 716, trapezoid 679, size 793, delete 2,127, big 2,982 targets) receive layouts from a constraint-aware, relation-preserving layout optimisation of the reference that keeps the reference's wall sides; they are used only as augmentation for placement, never for the keep, relation or addition heads.

Label fixes. The corpus preprocessing had labelled the 3D-FUTURE fine category “Footstool / Sofastool / Bed End Stool / Stool” as double bed (2,849 of 8,169 pieces; median footprint 0.30 m² against 4.72 m² for the beds). We relabel these pieces as misc. Other mixed labels remain: wardrobe includes “Bookcase / jewelry Armoire” (3,178 of 9,210); sofa includes “Lazy Sofa” (167 of 3,499); l sofa includes “Chaise Longue Sofa” (52 of 900). Asset substitution picks within the same fine category.

Training. The trunk is first trained for placement and then with all heads, on mixed batches of placement and analysis rows (AdamW, learning rate 0.0003, batch 64, EMA 0.999, bf16); the final stage runs 20,000 steps. Appendix A lists all settings and the training stages.

5. Experiments

5.1. Setup

Test sets. The main comparison (§5.2), the style test (§5.5) and the augmentation ablation of §5.4 use a test set frozen once from the held-out test rooms with fixed selection rules and never used for training or tuning (between the two models trained for 20,000 steps with and without the wall-side constraint in the augmentation, §5.4, the one shown is the one with the lower \(D_A\) on the validation real pairs, the primary metric: 0.082 against 0.084): 700 standard-test targets (50 rooms with 6–12 pieces including a bed, a sofa with a TV or a dining set; each scaled by 0.7×, 0.8×, 0.9×, 1.0×, 1.1×, 1.2×, 1.3× and reshaped into seven outlines no smaller than the reference) and 300 real pairs \((A,B)\), in which a method furnishes \(B\)'s empty room from \(A\). The set is stored with a sha256 manifest; each baseline was run once on it and its layouts are re-scored by the same code. The other ablations (§5.4) and the qualitative results (§5.3) use the validation split, whose standard tests have 10 rooms and whose real-pair set has 120 pairs.

Metrics. \(D\) and \(D_A\) (eq. 2, over kept pieces) measure how well the reference arrangement is kept; kept is the share of reference pieces in the output. Torn pairs: of the floor pairs touching in the reference (gap ≤ 5 cm) and both kept, the share more than 30 cm apart. Wall kept: the share of the reference's wall contacts (same side) that survive, for categories where wall contact matters. Out of room: floor pieces more than 5% outside; collision: floor pairs overlapping by more than 2% of the smaller footprint (10% on real pairs), nestable pairs excluded; door blocked: door boxes more than 5% covered. On real pairs, keep F1 compares the kept pieces of \(A\) with those designer \(B\) kept; add F1, precision and recall compare the added categories with \(B\)'s additions.

Baselines. Both use their released living-room checkpoints on all room types. LEGO-Net [6] receives the affine transplant of every reference piece its vocabulary covers (58% of the pieces on real pairs) with the target floor plan and denoises it with the authors' settings; other pieces stay at the transplant; it keeps everything and adds nothing. PhyScene [5] receives only the target floor plan — its released model has no object-list or arrangement conditioning — and samples with its collision, room-layout and reachability guidance; its objects are matched to \(A\)'s pieces by category (43% of \(A\)'s pieces matched on real pairs), and \(D\) is computed over the matched pieces.

Statistics. Differences are mean paired differences over the same targets or pairs, with 95% intervals and two-sided \(p\) from a bootstrap that resamples rooms or pairs (10,000 resamples). Appendix Table 11 lists all tests.

On real pairs IntentFlow keeps the reference arrangement at \(D_A=0.082\), against 0.184 for LEGO-Net (−0.102 [−0.111, −0.093], p<0.001, 300 pairs) and 0.386 for PhyScene (−0.298 [−0.316, −0.281], p<0.001, 214 pairs), with 0.3% of pieces out of the room and 6.7% colliding pairs (LEGO-Net 17.5% / 18.6%, PhyScene 15.9% / 13.2%). It keeps the pieces the designer kept as well as LEGO-Net, which keeps everything (p=0.828; IntentFlow's keep F1 differs on 55 of 300 pairs, higher on 29 and lower on 26), and better than PhyScene (p<0.001); its additions reach add F1 0.247 (precision 0.41, recall 0.15), above LEGO-Net (p<0.001) and PhyScene (p=0.010). It tears fewer groups than PhyScene (p=0.039); against LEGO-Net the difference is not significant (p=0.868).

On the standard tests IntentFlow's \(D\) is 0.060 / 0.066 (scale / outline), against 0.158 / 0.141 for LEGO-Net and 0.379 / 0.371 for PhyScene (p<0.001 in all four comparisons), and it keeps 0.84 / 0.73 of the reference's wall contacts (LEGO-Net 0.43 / 0.26, PhyScene 0.30 / 0.34). It tears fewer groups than PhyScene on both tests (p=0.002, p<0.001); against LEGO-Net the torn-pair differences are not significant (p=0.294, p=0.118). Its largest out-of-room, collision or door-blocking rate over all columns is 1.8%. The affine transplant keeps the arrangement best — \(D_A=0.069\) — but leaves 30.7% of pieces outside the room: \(D\) measures arrangement, not feasibility, and the gap between the transplant and IntentFlow is what feasibility costs.

6. Limitations

The keep head saturates for large pieces: when a bed and another large piece both get keep probabilities near 1 and the density budget cannot hold both, relation mass decides, and in very small bedrooms the bed can be dropped. IntentFlow adds fewer pieces than designers (1.34 against 3.02 per real pair, recall 0.15), and some additions are implausible, such as a second wardrobe beside the first. In reshaped rooms, keeping wall sides and keeping groups conflict, and the model inherits the wall-side preference of its augmentation; augmentation without the wall-side constraint gives up wall contacts significantly while its fewer torn pairs are not significant at 5% (§5.4), so neither choice resolves the conflict. The shared heads remain below separately trained networks. Mixed category labels remain in the corpus. The baselines are living-room checkpoints run on all room types, PhyScene cannot receive the reference, and LEGO-Net sees only pieces in its vocabulary. The pipeline takes 86 s per real pair on CPU (the structure step can place several candidates and each addition is placed and projected), whereas LEGO-Net takes 7 s and PhyScene 120 s under their own settings. Windows constrain nothing in \(\Pi_\theta\), and human clearances such as room to pull out a chair are not modelled.

7. Conclusion

IntentFlow moves a home's arrangement into another room with one transformer: from a shared attention trunk it decides what a designer keeps, how strongly each relation is held, what to add, and where every piece goes, and a projection in an arrangement metric makes the result feasible. Learned from pairs of real rooms furnished by two designers, it keeps the reference arrangement closer than LEGO-Net and PhyScene on a frozen held-out test set (\(D_A\) 0.082 against 0.184 and 0.386) with almost no pieces outside the room. Adding as many pieces as designers do, and resolving the conflict between walls and groups in irregular rooms, remain open.
