Reference-to-Room Arrangement Transfer with a Learned Relation Graph and Learned Inventory Editing

v0.7 · placement, relations and inventory editing in one model · every number on this page is computed from the result files of the evaluation runs

Abstract

We move a furnished room into another room: the reference room's own furniture and its arrangement are carried into an empty target room of a different size and outline, with its own doors and windows, and the inventory is thinned or completed when the target cannot hold it or has room to spare. One transformer does all of it. It places every reference piece (a LEGO-Net-style piece token [1] reading the target's boundary as Kim et al.'s point tokens [2]); it learns a relation graph over the pieces from data — for every pair, the relative pose a designer would give it and how fixed that pose is (heteroscedastic regression [3]) — with no relation types, no grouping rule and no distance threshold; and it decides the inventory edits with a position-free inventory branch (how many pieces of each category are too many or too few) and DETR-style add queries [4] (where an addition goes). A projection \(\Pi_\theta\) then makes the layout physically feasible, holding together only what the learned graph holds together. On 75 real room pairs that keep the reference's furniture, the method beats LEGO-Net [1], MiDiffusion [5] and PhyScene [6] on collision-freedom (CF), staying in bounds (IB) and recall of the reference's relations (iRecall); against LEGO-Net, the strongest at keeping relations, iRecall is 43/23, p=0.019 (rooms won/lost). In Kim et al.'s protocol (the target's own inventory) it wins every metric but the door clearance, where too few rooms have a door to decide. Its edits stay within a 10 % error: on an add / delete set built on the same pairs, it deletes 1.9% / 1.2% of the rooms' own pieces (bedroom / living room) and 98.1% / 100.0% of its additions are plausible.

1. Introduction

When people move, they rarely want a new arrangement: they want theirs, in the new room — the bed between its nightstands, the sofa facing the television across the coffee table, the dining set. The new room has another size, another outline and doors in other places, so the arrangement has to adapt; and a smaller room cannot take every piece, a larger one leaves space a designer would furnish. Kim et al. [2] study this transfer with the target room's own inventory and note the assumption of comparable inventories as a limitation. We keep the reference's furniture — the thesis of this work — and let the model edit the inventory when the room demands it.

Three things are learned from data rather than written as rules. Placement: where each reference piece goes in the target. Relations: which pieces belong together and how tightly, as a learned graph over the pieces; earlier versions grouped pieces by a 0.3 m distance rule, which this version removes entirely, from the training losses and from the projection. Inventory: which pieces go when the room is too small, and what is added when it has space. Only physics — no overlap beyond what designers allow, inside the room, door clearance — is a constraint, enforced by a projection.

Contributions: (i) a single model for placement, relations and inventory editing (Sec. 3); (ii) a relation graph learned as the uncertainty of each pair's relative pose, used both to weight the training's relation loss and as the pair term of the projection (Sec. 3.3); (iii) an inventory branch that decides edits without seeing positions, which removes the shortcut that made earlier versions delete pieces for starting at random places (Sec. 3.4); (iv) evaluation on real room pairs in the reference-furniture setting and in Kim et al.'s protocol, with sign tests against three published methods, and an add / delete set with answers (Sec. 5).

2. Related work

Scene synthesis. ATISS [7], DiffuScene [8], MiDiffusion [5] and PhyScene [6] generate rooms from a floor plan (MiDiffusion also from a list of objects); none takes an exemplar arrangement. Rearrangement. LEGO-Net [1] denoises a scrambled room back to a regular arrangement of the same objects; we take its piece tokens. Retargeting. Kim et al. [2] transfer an arrangement to a new room with the target's inventory, rule-based groups and a rule-free correction; their boundary point tokens and their metrics (CF, IB, iRecall) are ours. Set prediction and uncertainty. The add queries follow DETR [4]; the relation graph is a heteroscedastic regression [3]. All rooms come from 3D-FRONT [9] with 3D-FUTURE assets [10].

3. Method

3.1. Problem

Given a furnished reference room \(A\) (pieces with category, size and pose) and an empty target room \(B\) (outline, doors, windows), output a layout of \(A\)'s pieces in \(B\), possibly without some of them and with pieces added, that keeps \(A\)'s arrangement where \(B\) allows it and is physically feasible. The reference is framed into \(B\) first (centroid on centroid, positions scaled by the square root of the area ratio); every head reads that framed input. Fig. 1 follows one pair through the method.

3.2. Placement

Each piece is one token (category embedding, size and elevation, Fourier features of its framed position, its orientation), as LEGO-Net [1]; the target's boundary is 250 points with inward normals plus one token per door or window, read by cross-attention as Kim et al. [2]. Six decoder blocks output, per piece, a position (the input plus an offset; a mixture of eight offsets, winner-take-all) and a turn (36 classes plus an offset within the class). Two passes refine the layout. Fig. 2 shows the whole network.

3.3. The learned relation graph

For every ordered pair \((i, j)\) a head predicts the relative pose of \(j\) in \(i\)'s frame (offset and turn) and its standard deviation \(\sigma_{ij}\), trained by the Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the designer's answer [3]. A pair the data places the same way every time gets a small \(\sigma\): the edge weight \(w_{ij}\) is the probability, under the learned \(\sigma_{ij}\), that the pair keeps its relative position within 10 cm. The graph has no relation types and no threshold on distance or category. It is used twice: the training's relation loss covers every pair, each weighted by \(w_{ij}\) (earlier versions selected "dependents" by a 0.3 m / three-times-the-area rule), and the projection's pair term is weighted by it (earlier versions used rigid groups of pieces within 0.3 m and a table of category keep-rates). In the validation rooms the dining chairs' strongest edge is to their dining table (mean weight 0.218, against 0.049 to the coffee table), and a nightstand's edge to its bed is 0.30 of its strongest against 0.28 for the wardrobe (Fig. 3).

3.4. Inventory editing

Whether to edit is decided by an inventory branch that reads only the pieces' categories and sizes and the target room, never a position: for each category of the inventory, how many pieces are too many (the votes of its pieces averaged), and for each category of the vocabulary, how many are missing. An earlier version decided deletions per piece from the placement tokens; it learned that a piece at an odd place is one to delete, and deleted 9.9% / 12.3% of the rooms' own pieces (bedroom / living room) on the add / delete set, because a piece without a partner in the reference starts at a random place just as an intruder does. Which piece and where come from the placement branch: of a category with \(k\) too many, the \(k\) pieces with the lowest keep logit go; an addition takes the most probable of eight add queries (DETR-style set prediction [4], Hungarian matching), its count the rounded sum of their existence probabilities. A self-check then re-reads the inventory with the additions through the inventory branch and withdraws an addition it would delete. The pieces never attend to the add queries, so without edits the placement is unchanged. Deletions are taken at an operating point \(\tau = 0.8\) on the excess probability; the addition decoding (from the queries) was chosen on a selection set of other validation references (Table 7). The self-check is the exception: it was added after the evaluation pairs' bedrooms showed a third nightstand added next to two, and on the selection set it does not help (Table 7), so Table 6's additions are not free of that one look at the evaluation pairs. Fig. 4 shows how the threshold trades deletions against false deletions on validation edits.

3.5. Feasibility projection \(\Pi_\theta\)

An augmented-Lagrangian projection moves the pieces as little as possible so that no two footprints overlap, every piece is inside the room and door clearances are free, while a pair term keeps the model's relative poses, weighted by the learned edges. It has no grouping rule: no distance groups, no anchors by size, no rule for tall or mirrored pieces.

4. Data and training

Training rows are real designed rooms, with no cross-room answers: a room with its pieces perturbed (LEGO-Net's online perturbation [1]), and a room's arrangement carried into another room's real outline and framed back. A share of the reference poses is corrupted as real pairs are (a piece takes another piece's pose, is turned, or starts at a random place). Edits are generated online, on the user's rule: to teach adding, one to three of a room's pieces are taken out and the room as designed is the answer; to teach deleting, one to three pieces that make the inventory too much — a duplicate fewer than 5 % of the training rooms of the type hold, or a piece that takes the furniture's floor share above the type's 95th percentile — are put in at random places, and deleting them is the answer; the deletion labels are counts per category. The inventory heads are added to a placement-only model and trained with it; its placement is kept by distillation to that model on the unedited inputs (Learning without Forgetting [11]). One model per room type; the physics term allows the designers' own overlaps.

5. Experiments

5.1. Setup

Real pairs, reference furniture (our setting). The 75 Kim-style pairs (50 living rooms, 25 bedrooms; a reference and the same-type validation room of another house with the most similar inventory): the reference's furniture is carried into the target. The baselines get the same furniture: LEGO-Net denoises from the framed reference, MiDiffusion reads its categories and the floor plan, PhyScene (living rooms only, its released model) reads the floor plan. Kim et al.'s protocol. The same pairs with the target's own inventory placed at its reference partners' poses (no edits are expected). Metrics. CF: no two floor footprints overlap by more than 2 % of the smaller; CFk: overlaps the designers make allowed (Kim et al.); IB: every piece inside; iRecall: the reference's relations recalled; door: door clearance boxes blocked. Sign tests count the rooms where one method is better (ties dropped).

6. Limitations and conclusion

6.1. Limitations

  • The rule-free projection pushes dining chairs out from under their tables (Sec. 5.6).
  • In shrunk targets the model also adds pieces (Table 8).
  • The learned edges are small in absolute value: the relation head reads the framed, corrupted input, so its uncertainty includes the input's noise; a head on the position-free branch learns sharper edges but has not yet given better layouts.
  • A data-augmentation error was found in the training (a mirrored room's pieces were turned by a half turn); the reported model was trained with it. Retrained models learn sharper relations but do not yet match the reported layouts.
  • The kim75 pairs are validation rooms; a test-split set for final numbers is still to be built.
  • \(\Pi_\theta\) is not bit-reproducible across runs: the runtime run of Table 11 (the same model and settings, one process) gives living-room CF 0.92 / IB 0.88 / iRecall 0.843 against Table 1's 0.88 / 0.80 / 0.843; the tables report the first run.

6.2. Conclusion

One model places a reference room's furniture in a new room, learns which pieces belong together from data, and edits the inventory within a 10 % error; with only physics as a constraint, it beats three published methods on the real pairs, keeping the reference's furniture and in Kim et al.'s protocol.
