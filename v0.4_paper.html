IntentFlow: Retargeting a Furnished Room into a New Floor Plan, Learned from Designers' Room Pairs

Abstract. Given a furnished reference room and an empty target room of another size or shape, we place the reference's furniture in the target so that the designer's arrangement survives: the same pieces where they fit, the same relations between them, pieces dropped when the room shrinks and pieces added when it grows. All design decisions are learned from 68,958 pairs of real 3D-FRONT rooms [1]: pairs of rooms that share furniture, pairs where a designer furnished a smaller or larger room from the same set, and rooms with part of their furniture masked out. A rectified flow [2] transports the reference layout, framed into the target room, to the target layout; a state head decides whether the room loses, keeps or gains furniture and a next-action head which pieces to drop, re-categorise or add; a style head chooses the assets, and a single scalar λ moves them from the reference's style towards another style. A mesh-level augmented-Lagrangian projection [3] enforces physics (no overlap, inside the room) and restores what the reference itself prescribes (its close groups, its walls, its angles), with every tolerance taken from real rooms; among K candidate decisions from the model the most probable one the projection can make feasible is kept. On a held-out test split (700 targets: 7 scales and 7 shapes of 100 reference rooms) IntentFlow keeps 0.76 of the reference's close relations (LEGO-Net [4] 0.60, MiDiffusion [5] 0.31, PhyScene [6] 0.13), with relation distance D 0.065 (baselines 0.160–0.356) and 0.3% of pieces outside the room (baselines 11.6%–16.5%); its layouts also have the lowest FID / KID against the split's real rooms (15.6 / 5.12) and the highest walkable / reachable shares (0.967 / 0.957). Without a style change 0.955 of the pieces keep the reference's style; at λ = 1, 0.45–0.60 carry the target style while the arrangement is unchanged.

1 Introduction

Moving a furnished room into another floor plan is a common design task: a family moves to a smaller flat, a showroom is repeated in a building with other walls. The new layout should be recognisably the same design: the bed with its nightstands, the sofa facing the television across the coffee table, the chairs around the table, while fitting walls, doors and windows that the reference never had. Scene synthesis models such as ATISS [7], DiffuScene [8], PhyScene [6] and MiDiffusion [5] generate a room from its floor plan but do not take a reference layout as input, so the design they produce is a new one; LEGO-Net [4] rearranges a given set of pieces towards a regular layout but keeps every piece and cannot add or drop furniture.

We learn the task from what designers did. 3D-FRONT [1] contains many rooms furnished from the same set of 3D-FUTURE [9] assets; two such rooms form a pair (A, B) in which a designer has carried a layout into another room. Where B is smaller the designer dropped pieces, where it is larger they added some, and the relations they kept or broke are the supervision for every design decision. Our contributions:

  • A formulation of layout retargeting as a learned transport from the reference layout, framed into the target room, to the target layout (rectified flow), trained on real designer pairs and on masked rooms, with no hand-written design rule.
  • A next-action head that edits the reference set (drop / change category / add with an anchor piece / stop), learned from the pairs' edit sequences, and a feasible-MAP search: K decision sequences sampled from the model, each placed by the flow and projected, the most probable feasible one kept. Physics is the only constraint and is applied as a projection, not as a training penalty.
  • A style head with a scalar degree of change λ: at λ = 0 the reference's assets and style are kept; increasing λ moves the assets towards a target style while the layout stays fixed.
  • An evaluation on a frozen, held-out test split against LEGO-Net, PhyScene and MiDiffusion on the same targets, with transfer metrics (relations kept, relation distance, walls kept, pieces out of the room, collisions) and PhyScene's realism metrics.

2 Related work

Scene synthesis. ATISS [7] places pieces autoregressively; DiffuScene [8] and MiDiffusion [5] denoise the whole set (MiDiffusion with mixed discrete categories and continuous boxes, conditioned on the floor plan); PhyScene [6] adds physical guidance (collision, room boundary, reachability). All generate a room from its outline; none is conditioned on a reference layout, which is what our task requires. Rearrangement. LEGO-Net [4] moves a given set of pieces towards a regular layout by learned denoising; the set is fixed. Flows. Rectified flow [2] and flow matching [10] learn a velocity field between two distributions; stochastic interpolants [11] add a noise term along the path. Because we start the flow from the reference layout instead of noise, the transport is short where the room changes little and the reference's design is the default. Masked training. Masking part of the input and predicting it [12] gives a dense self-supervised signal; we mask furniture to teach the model what a room is missing and where it belongs.

3 Data: designers' room pairs

Rooms come from 3D-FRONT [1] with their 3D-FUTURE [9] assets, split by house (train / validation / test). Ceiling and pendant lamps are removed. A pair (A, B) holds a reference A and a target B furnished by a designer; we use

  • transfer pairs: A and B share furniture and B's arrangement of the shared pieces is close to A's (relation distance D below 0.15), plus self pairs (A = B);
  • downsizing / upsizing pairs: B is smaller (larger) than A and furnished from A's set with pieces dropped (added);
  • masked rooms: a self pair with 1 to 30% of its pieces removed from A; those pieces become B's added pieces at their own positions, so the model learns which piece a room lacks and where it goes.

The training set has 68,958 pairs (validation 293). Every decision the model makes (keep or drop a piece, change its category, add a piece of a category next to a given piece, keep the asset) has a target in these pairs.

4 Method

4.1 Representation

A layout is a set of slots. Slot $i \le N_F$ holds reference piece $i$ (existence, category one-hot $c_i$, log size, elevation, position $p_i$, orientation $(\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$, appearance $a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$); $M = 8$ further slots hold added pieces. The rooms are tokens too: wall segments and openings (doors, windows) of A and of B, and B's room type. The reference is framed into B before the model reads it: A's positions are scaled by $\sqrt{|B| / |A|}$ about the room centres (sizes and orientations kept), so copying the reference means keeping it in the new room.

4.2 Transfer flow

Let $x_0$ be the framed reference (added slots: Gaussian noise) and $x_1$ the designer's layout of B. A transformer ($L=8$ layers, width 256, 8.2 M parameters) predicts the velocity $v_\phi(x_t, t \mid A, B)$ and is trained with the rectified-flow loss [2] $$\mathcal{L}_\text{flow} = \mathbb{E}_{t, (A,B)} \big\| v_\phi(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \big\|^2,\quad x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 ,$$ with categorical and box dimensions averaged separately. During training the reference is perturbed by one random scale per scene (position noise $\sigma_p \sim |\mathcal N(0, 0.15)|$, orientation noise $\sigma_\theta \sim |\mathcal N(0, \pi/4)|$), so the flow learns to repair a displaced layout; at inference it starts from the reference itself. An added piece is generated in the frame of its anchor piece (position offset and relative orientation), so "a nightstand beside the bed" is one learned offset regardless of where the bed stands. Existence and category of every slot are decided before the flow (4.3) and given to it from $t=0$. The source of an added piece is not Gaussian noise but a pose drawn from the data: the relative pose (size, offset, relative orientation) of a random training piece of the same category next to an anchor of the same category (79,939 poses over 816 category pairs, from the training split only). As A's pieces start from the reference, an added piece thus starts from how designers place such a piece next to such an anchor, and the flow moves it to where this room needs it; a Gaussian source left the added pieces' distribution about 2 m wide (Section 5.4).

4.3 Decisions: the room's state, hotspots and the next edit

State head. Read on the reference as it stands in the target room, a classifier predicts the room's state $s \in \{\text{delete}, \text{keep}, \text{add}\}$: delete when the designer's room has fewer pieces than the reference, add when it has more, keep otherwise (masked rooms are "add"). Hotspots. Two maps over a $16\times16$ grid on the target floor give where designers added pieces ($h^+$) and where the reference pieces they deleted stood ($h^-$): every cell is a query that attends to all scene tokens, trained with cross-entropy against the cells of the designer's added pieces and of the deleted reference pieces. Next-action head. Given the current furniture set it outputs one distribution over the next edit: drop piece $i$, change piece $i$ to category $c$, add a piece of category $c$ (with an anchor piece chosen by an anchor head), or stop. It is trained on random intermediate states between A's set and B's set (each edit applied with probability $u \sim U(0,1)$); the target is any edit still to do, or stop. The state constrains the edits: delete allows only drops (at least one), keep allows neither drops nor additions, add allows only additions (at least one); a drop's logit is raised by $\log h^-$ at the piece's cell. A category change (a substitute) is allowed only in the delete state, where the room loses furniture, and only within the dataset's own super-category (3D-FUTURE: bed, sofa, chair, table, cabinet/shelf/desk, ...); in training, a change across super-categories (a sofa matched to a bed by position) is learned as a drop plus an addition. 4.4 Assets and style

A same-asset head decides whether a kept piece keeps its own 3D-FUTURE asset; otherwise a style head predicts an appearance vector and the asset is retrieved jointly by appearance and size. The style label is an input of the style head only (15% dropped in training); the degree of change $\lambda$ moves the predicted appearance from the reference's own style $a_\text{ref}$ towards a target style: $a = a_\text{ref} + \lambda\,(a_\text{tgt} - a_\text{ref})$, the classifier-free guidance form [13]. The layout does not depend on λ.

4.5 Physics and the feasible-MAP search

Pieces are projected onto a feasible set by an augmented Lagrangian [3] over positions (25 outer × 100 inner iterations). Physics is the only hard rule; every tolerance comes from real rooms or from an evaluation metric's own definition:

  • No interpenetration of the pieces' mesh cross-sections at any height, and every piece inside the room.
  • Top-view overlap: two floor pieces of categories $(a, b)$ may overlap in top view only as deep as real rooms let such a pair overlap, the 90th percentile of the separating-axis penetration of their footprints over the training rooms (91 pairs above 1 cm, e.g. dining chair under dining table 0.38 m; chair on chair, coffee table on dining chair, bed on bed 0).
  • Orientation: a kept piece is turned to its own reference angle to the wall it stands against (orientation is part of the design that is transferred, as in Scene Retargeting's rotation term); an added piece is turned to square (or 45°) when that is the maximum a-posteriori angle under its category's real deviation prior (training rooms, 1° bins) and the flow's measured orientation noise (raw outputs of reference-square pieces, test-time measurement on validation targets), and keeps its generated angle otherwise (armchairs are often angled, beds never). "Square" is relative to the wall the piece stands against or to the room's dominant wall direction, whichever needs the smaller turn (the nearest orientation that is square to either), so a free piece, or one that meets a cut corner with a single corner, is not turned towards a slanted wall. A reference angle within 6° of square counts as square: in the training rooms 92.5% of floor pieces lie within 1° and 6° is the emptiest bin before the angled ones.
  • Relations within groups: pieces that touched in the reference form a group; every kept member is put back at its reference offset and relative angle to the group's largest piece, which stays where the flow put it (the reference's own close relations, as the relation term to a cluster anchor in Scene Retargeting). The flow keeps groups together when the room keeps its size but spreads them when it grows; this step restores them, and the solve then removes any overlap it creates.
  • Wall contact of groups: pieces that touched in the reference form a group; a group with a member that stood against a wall is moved rigidly along that wall's normal until the member is within its category's real wall gap (90th percentile of wall-backed gaps in the training rooms; contact as defined by the wall-kept metric, 0.15 m). Moving pieces one by one instead tears groups apart.
  • Door clearance: no floor piece in the clearance box in front of a door (the door-blocked metric's box, ≥ 1 m wide, 0.9 m deep).
  • Reachability (PhyScene's walkable raster [6]): robot-width squares along the cheapest paths that join every door and every free region to the largest free region are added as obstacles for pieces below 1.5 m and the projection is repeated (≤ 4 rounds, the most walkable round that stays feasible is kept).

Feasibility, which the search below uses, is decided by physics and the top-view tolerance only; door clearance and the path squares are pursued by the same optimisation but do not decide feasibility (a local optimum between two door boxes would otherwise leave only the empty room "feasible"). Assets are retrieved jointly by appearance and size, and never larger than the footprint the model predicted (beyond the 2 cm feasibility tolerance) while a fitting asset of the category exists.

We search K = 16 candidates: four take the most probable state and edits (with different placements of the added pieces; one of them without category changes), two take the other states, and the rest carry the most probable sequence on by 1, 2, ... further edits of the head's own ranking (stop not allowed before), alternately with and without category changes (a substituted piece may be larger than the reference's own). Each candidate is placed by the flow and projected (reachability rounds only for the kept candidate); we keep the candidate of highest log-probability whose residual violation is at most 2 cm, and among equally probable ones the one the projection moved least. A room too small for the most probable decisions therefore receives the model's own next deletions until physics can fit it; how many pieces are deleted is not set by any rule. A per-piece wall-contact constraint was tried first: it keeps every wall but tears groups apart (Table 9, row K); moving whole groups keeps them.

5 Experiments

5.1 Protocol

The test split is frozen before any model is evaluated on it: 700 targets from 100 reference rooms (scale test: the reference's own outline at 0.7–1.3×; shape test: stretched, cut, L-, T-, pentagon-, trapezoid- and long rooms no smaller than the reference), 300 real designer pairs and 50 rooms for the style test. Baselines are run once on the same targets with their released models: LEGO-Net moves the reference pieces from the affine transplant; PhyScene and MiDiffusion generate a room from the target outline, and their objects are matched to the reference pieces by category. Metrics: kept (share of reference pieces in the output), rel. kept (reference pairs within 0.3 m that stay within 0.3 m with the same relative orientation and side), D (relation distance over kept pairs: direction, relative orientation, log distance ratio, contact), iRecall (share of the reference's in-front-of / beside / facing / aligned / against-wall triplets realised), wall kept, square (pieces square to their nearest wall within 5°), torn (touching reference pairs pulled more than 30 cm apart), out (pieces more than 5% outside), collide (floor pairs overlapping more than 2% of the smaller), same asset.

6 Limitations

  • Added pieces are placed from a wide learned distribution; the search picks among K placements, but a piece may still go to the wrong wall (Table 6 position error 0.82 m).
  • Pieces whose category the heads change (e.g. a double bed to a kids bed in a small room) are not always the designer's choice.
  • The out-of-room comparison favours methods with a projection; real rooms themselves violate the outline (Table 4).
  • Walkability after projection is below the unprojected flow on real pairs (Table 5): the projection separates pieces but does not keep a path free.
