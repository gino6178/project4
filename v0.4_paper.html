Abstract. Transferring an established interior arrangement from a reference room to an empty target room — under arbitrary scaling, non-congruent polygonal boundaries, and mismatched door/window configurations — requires joint continuous spatial retargeting and discrete furniture editing while preserving relational design intent. Existing generative scene synthesizers either sample layouts from scratch or violate hard architectural boundaries through their regression errors. We present IntentFlow, a framework that unifies discrete set editing, continuous generative transport, and constrained geometric optimization. Conditioned on the framed reference and the architectural boundaries, a single transformer trunk drives a state head, delete and add hotspots, and a sequential next-action policy, learned from 68,958 pairs of real 3D-FRONT rooms [1]. A rectified-flow [2] velocity head transports the retargeted furniture, and discrete spatial adaptation is formulated as constrained decoding over 16 candidate edit plans, which resolves capacity limits without hand-crafted density budgets. To satisfy hard architectural boundaries without diluting the learned intent, an augmented Lagrangian projection \(\Pi_\theta\) [3] enforces mesh-level non-penetration, containment, door clearance and reachability on the non-convex feasible set, with top-view overlap allowances measured in real rooms, while restoring the reference-prescribed groups, wall contacts and orientations. A style head disentangles spatial arrangement from visual appearance, enabling stylistic asset substitution with small layout drift (\(D\le0.034\)). On a frozen held-out benchmark of 700 rescaled and reshaped targets, IntentFlow reaches the lowest relation distance of the compared methods, \(D=0.059\), and retains 0.69 of the reference's close relations, outperforming LEGO-Net [4] (0.51 / 0.149), MiDiffusion [5] (0.26 / 0.300) and PhyScene [6] (0.15 / 0.348; all p<0.001). IntentFlow leaves 0.2% of its pieces outside the room (10.8%–13.0% for the baselines), has the lowest FID / KID of the compared methods against the split's real rooms on these targets (15.6 / 5.12), the highest walkable / reachable shares (0.967 / 0.957), and runs in 6.5 s median per target on a single GPU.

1. Introduction

When furnishing a new living space, occupants frequently seek to transfer a proven interior arrangement from a reference home into their own — the bed between its nightstands, the sofa facing the television across the coffee table, the chairs around the dining table. However, target rooms typically exhibit divergent floor dimensions, non-congruent polygonal outlines, and mismatched door and window locations (Fig. 1). An expert interior designer addressing this challenge does not generate a layout from scratch; rather, they perform reference-to-room arrangement transfer: determining which signature pieces to retain, dropping or swapping secondary items that no longer fit, introducing complementary additions called for by the new room, and adjusting continuous poses so that functional clusters, passages, and wall orientations are preserved. Formally, given a furnished reference room \(A\) and an empty target room \(B\), the goal is to synthesize a populated layout in \(B\) that preserves the relational design intent of \(A\) while remaining physically feasible and usable.

This task is challenging due to four coupled factors. First, discrete and continuous decisions interact: spatial feasibility dictates which items can fit, yet spatial placement itself depends on which items are retained. Second, design intent is not a static list of categorical relations; spatial relations deform under aspect-ratio changes, and which of them should survive depends on the target boundaries. Third, real-world retargeting often requires layout-appearance disentanglement: homeowners frequently wish to renew the furniture's appearance (e.g., moving to another furniture style) while maintaining the familiar spatial grouping. Fourth, the generated scene must respect hard architectural constraints — preventing interpenetration, keeping every piece inside the walls and out of the doorways, and leaving a free path to every piece — which unconstrained neural generators meet only up to their regression error.

Prior indoor scene generators [7] [8] [9] [6] [5] sample plausible layouts conditioned on boundaries, object lists or text, but discard the specific spatial arrangement of a reference scene; MiDiffusion can be given the reference's objects, but not their arrangement. Rearrangement models such as LEGO-Net [4] iteratively regularize messy layouts toward corpus distributions rather than transferring intent across non-isomorphic spaces. Layout optimization methods [10] [11] optimize hand-crafted energy terms without learning from data which piece a designer gives up, what they add, or which wall a group belongs to.

We present IntentFlow, a framework that learns these decisions from designers and keeps physics as a projection (Fig. 2). In 3D-FRONT [1] many rooms are furnished from the same 3D-FUTURE [12] assets: two such rooms form a pair in which a designer has carried a layout into another room, given up pieces where it was smaller and added pieces where it was larger. Together with rooms whose furniture we partly mask out, these pairs supervise every decision. The reference is framed into the target room and processed by one attention trunk alongside the walls and openings of both rooms (Fig. 3). Read on the framed reference, a state head decides whether the room loses, keeps or gains furniture, two hotspot maps say where designers delete and add, and a next-action head edits the set one step at a time — drop, change category, add next to an anchor, stop. Along the flow, a rectified-flow velocity head transports the edited set to its place; a same-asset head and a style head choose the 3D assets, with one degree of change towards a target style. Finally, to guarantee physical validity without diluting the learned intent, a mesh-level augmented Lagrangian projection \(\Pi_\theta\) enforces non-penetration, containment, door clearance and reachability, and restores what the reference itself prescribes: its touching groups, its wall contacts and its angles. How many pieces a smaller room loses is not set by any rule: among 16 candidate plans the model's most probable one that \(\Pi_\theta\) can make feasible is kept. Table 1 lists which decisions are learned and which are rules, and where every constant comes from.

Our principal contributions are:

  • A learned edit plan (§3.3). We formulate spatial adaptation as learning designers' sequential edits: a state head, delete and add hotspots and a next-action head, trained on the edits of 68,958 real pairs and masked rooms, decide what the room loses, keeps or gains, and a search over 16 plans lets physics decide only how far the plan must go. On the 0.7× targets IntentFlow keeps 0.63 of the pieces with 0.0% out of the room, where methods that keep every piece leave 50.6%–52.1% out (Table 5); in masked completion it adds the removed category in 0.33 of the rooms, against 0.24 for the room type's most frequent category (§5.5).
  • Placement as transport from the framed reference (§3.2). We formulate arrangement retargeting as a rectified flow from the reference framed into the target, conditioned on both rooms, so that keeping the reference is the default and the transport is short where the room changes little; added pieces start from how designers place their category next to their anchor. On the test split IntentFlow keeps 0.69 of the close relations and tears 0.064 of the touching pairs (LEGO-Net 0.179, MiDiffusion 0.207, PhyScene 0.334; §5.2).
  • Reference-prescribed feasibility projection \(\Pi_\theta\) (§3.4). An augmented Lagrangian solver projects every candidate onto hard constraints — non-penetration of the meshes' cross-sections with top-view overlap allowances measured in real rooms, containment, door clearance and reachability — after restoring the reference's groups, wall contacts and angles. Out-of-room pieces fall to 0.2% while 0.90 of the reference's wall contacts are kept (baselines 0.45–0.67).
  • Layout-appearance disentanglement (§3.5). The trunk never sees the style label; a style head with one scalar \(\lambda\) moves the assets from the reference's style towards a target style, so the appearance changes while the arrangement drifts by \(D\le0.034\) (§5.6).
  • Objective evaluation (§5). On a frozen held-out test split (700 targets, 285 real pairs, 200 completion rooms, 50 style rooms), against LEGO-Net, PhyScene and MiDiffusion with paired bootstrap tests over rooms, IntentFlow reaches \(D=0.059\) against 0.149–0.348 and is closer to the split's real rooms by PhyScene's realism metrics than the three baselines (FID 15.6 against 17.1–23.6).

2. Related Work

Indoor scene synthesis. Generative modeling of indoor layouts has advanced through autoregressive transformers [7] [8], denoising diffusion models [9], and semantic graph conditioning [13] [14]. To enforce physical plausibility, PhyScene [6] introduces collision, room-layout and reachability guidance during diffusion sampling. Recent frameworks leverage LLMs and vision-language models for layout planning [15] [16] or optimize layouts against model-proposed relations [17]. MiDiffusion [5] mixes discrete categories with continuous boxes, addresses scene completion and arranges pre-specified object sets within a given floor plan. Classic approaches optimize hand-crafted interior design guidelines by stochastic search [10] [11] or sample from spatial statistics learned from a few exemplar scenes [18]. These methods model a layout given a room; a reference scene enters at most as a category list or a semantic graph, so its continuous geometry, wall relations and spatial grouping are not transferred.

Scene rearrangement. Rearrangement models transform disordered scenes into structured ones. LEGO-Net [4] learns to iteratively denoise messy furniture configurations into regular layouts. Given the transplanted reference layout, it moves furniture toward the arrangements of its training corpus rather than preserving the reference, it keeps every piece, and since it does not receive the room's walls and doors as constraints, pieces end across walls or in doorways when the target outline departs from the reference (§5.2).

Spatial relations and set editing. Relational layout pipelines typically plan a scene graph over a fixed vocabulary of predicates (e.g., surrounding, against, supported-by) and then instantiate boxes [19] [14]. Such categorical abstractions are brittle under continuous deformations, where distances and orientations warp non-uniformly. IntentFlow uses no relation categories: every pair of the reference's pieces receives the same continuous geometric descriptor, injected as an additive attention bias as in Graphormer [20], and the groups the projection restores are the reference's own touching pairs, not the output of a relation classifier. On the discrete side, adapting to a room requires reasoning about how many pieces a set holds. Related to set-based architectures [21] [22] and masked modelling [23], our next-action head treats downsizing and additions as sequential edits of a set with an explicit stop, learned on random intermediate states between a reference set and a designer's set, and masking furniture out of real rooms teaches it what a room lacks and where it belongs.

Flow matching and constrained optimization. Flow matching [24] and rectified flow [2] regress velocity fields along straight paths between two distributions, and stochastic interpolants [25] add a noise term along the path. Where standard rectified flows transport Gaussian noise to data, IntentFlow starts from an informative source, the reference framed into the target, and an added piece starts from a designer's pose of its category next to its anchor; sampling uses 100 Euler steps. The backbone is a permutation-equivariant transformer [26]. Rather than relying on soft penalty losses during generation, we decouple physical validity from learning: non-penetration, containment, door clearance and reachability are resolved by an augmented Lagrangian solver [3], which projects each sample onto the non-convex feasible set.

3. Method

IntentFlow treats arrangement transfer as a discrete edit of the furniture set, a continuous transport of the edited set and a constrained projection (Fig. 2). One attention trunk of 8 blocks processes the reference's furniture together with the walls and openings of both rooms and the slots of the layout being generated (Fig. 3). The design intent is not classified as a static graph: the reference's pairwise geometry biases the attention, the plan heads decide what the room keeps, and what the reference prescribes — its touching groups, its wall contacts, its angles — is restored before \(\Pi_\theta\) makes the layout feasible; among 16 candidate plans the most probable feasible one is kept.

A walkthrough. To illustrate the pipeline, Fig. 2 traces one target of the test split, drawn as typical (its \(D\) is the median of the 0.8× targets that lose furniture and gain none): a 26.9 m² bedroom shrunk to 17.2 m². Framed into the target, the reference no longer fits (Fig. 2b). Read once on the framed reference, the state head gives delete with probability 1.00 (keep 0.00, add 0.00); the delete hotspot is high where the pieces a designer would give up stand, and the next-action head drops the desk, the drawer chest, the stool and the shelf, reading the trunk again after every edit, until stopping is more likely than any further edit. The same network then moves the kept pieces from the framed reference to their places in 100 Euler steps (Fig. 2c), and \(\Pi_\theta\) returns the nearest feasible layout after restoring the reference's touching groups and squaring the pieces to their walls (Fig. 2d). Of the 16 candidates (Fig. 2e) — the most probable plan placed four times, the plans of the other two states, and the most probable plan carried on by further edits — 16 are feasible; the one kept has the highest log-probability, −1.37.

3.1. Problem formulation and arrangement distance

Problem definition. A reference room is a set of \(N\) placed pieces \(A=\{(c_i,s_i,p_i,\theta_i,k_i)\}_{i=1}^N\), where \(c_i\) is the category, \(s_i\in\mathbb R^3_{>0}\) the size of the bounding box, \(p_i\in\mathbb R^2\) the floor position, \(\theta_i\in[-\pi,\pi)\) the yaw and \(k_i\) a 3D-FUTURE asset [12]. The empty target room \(B\) is a simple polygon \(P_B\) with the segments of its doors and windows; the doors' clearance boxes \(\mathcal D\) enter the projection (Eq. 7). Optionally a target style and a degree of change \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) are given. The output is a layout \(\{(c'_j,s'_j,p'_j,\theta'_j,k'_j)\}_{j=1}^{N'}\) in \(P_B\) that keeps the groupings, orientations and wall relations of \(A\), with pieces dropped, changed to another category of their kind (a single bed for a double bed), restyled, or added (\(N'\neq N\) in general), under hard physical constraints.

Relation descriptor. Instead of discrete relation categories (e.g. left-of, facing), the relation of an ordered pair \((u,v)\) is a continuous descriptor \(\phi_{uv}\in\mathbb R^6\): the offset \(\Delta_{uv}=p_v-p_u\) in \(u\)'s right / front frame, the cosine and sine of their relative yaw, the gap between the two footprints and the centre distance.

Arrangement distance. The per-relation distance

$$\begin{aligned}\phi_{uv}&=\big(\Delta_{uv}\!\cdot\!\mathbf r_u,\ \Delta_{uv}\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_u,\ \cos(\theta_v-\theta_u),\ \sin(\theta_v-\theta_u),\ \mathrm{gap}(u,v),\ \lVert\Delta_{uv}\rVert\big),\\ d(\phi,\phi')&=\tfrac{1}{3.5}\Big[\tfrac{1-\cos\angle(\phi_{1:2},\phi'_{1:2})}{2}+\tfrac{1-(\phi_3\phi'_3+\phi_4\phi'_4)}{2}+\min\!\Big(\tfrac{|\log(\phi'_6/\phi_6)|}{\log 3},1\Big)+\tfrac12\big[\,[\phi_5<0.05]\neq[\phi'_5<0.05]\,\big]\Big]\in[0,1]\end{aligned}\tag{1}$$

penalises four distinct distortions: the direction of \(v\) as seen from \(u\); the orientation of \(v\) relative to \(u\) (for example, a coffee table turning away from its sofa); the ratio of their distances on a log scale, sensitive to changes between close pieces and saturating at 3× for distant ones; and whether they touch (gap below 5 cm), a discrete penalty when a contact is torn or created. The terms are invariant to moving and rotating the whole layout and lie in \([0,1]\): unlike a Chamfer distance or an IoU of footprints, the measure reacts to a turned piece and to a lost contact of a few centimetres. Between two layouts of corresponding pieces,

$$D(S,S')=\frac{1}{|\mathcal E|}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal E}d\big(\phi_{uv},\phi'_{uv}\big),\tag{2}$$

over all ordered pairs \(\mathcal E\) of pieces present in both. We write \(D\) for the distance of an output to its reference. \(D\) is a measure, not a training loss: we use it to evaluate (§5) and, with the threshold 0.15, to select the real pairs whose placement is supervised (§4).

3.2. Attention trunk and flow matching

Framing. IntentFlow starts from an informative source rather than from noise. The model never reads the reference in its own room: positions, walls and openings of \(A\) are carried into \(B\)'s frame about the rooms' centroids \(c_A, c_B\), isotropically by the square root of the area ratio, while sizes and orientations are kept:

$$\tilde p_i=c_B+\sqrt{|B|/|A|}\,\big(p_i-c_A\big),\qquad \tilde s_i=s_i,\qquad \tilde\theta_i=\theta_i,\tag{3}$$

so copying the framed reference means keeping the reference in the new room, and the flow's transport is short where the room changes little.

Tokens. A layout is a set of 24 reference slots and 8 added slots; each slot holds existence, category, log size, elevation, position and orientation (\(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\)) and a 32-d appearance vector, with positions in units of 4 m and Fourier features. The reference's pieces (category, size, framed pose, appearance), the wall segments and openings of both rooms (up to 24 walls and 8 openings per room, with an embedding of the room they belong to) and a condition token are tokens of the same attention. The condition token carries the target's room type and the relation distance asked for between the designer's layout and \(A\) — in training the pair's own \(D\), at inference 0, to keep the reference's arrangement; the plan heads read it as unknown, as in their training. A reference slot also receives its own reference piece's token, an added slot its anchor's token, and every slot a 7×7 occupancy view of the target's floor around its current position; the log areas of both rooms are added to every token and the flow time to the slot tokens.

Relational attention bias. The reference's pairwise geometry enters the attention logits [20]: for every head \(h\), \(\operatorname{softmax}\big(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k}+B^{(h)}\big)\) with \(B^{(h)}_{ij}=\mathrm{MLP}(e_{ij})_h\) for two pieces of \(A\), where \(e_{ij}\) holds \(j\)'s offset in \(i\)'s frame, their relative yaw and their distance. The trunk (Fig. 3) has 8 pre-norm blocks of width 256 with 8 heads (8,242,339 parameters) and is permutation-equivariant; the answer never enters an input.

Rectified flow on layouts. Placement is a rectified flow [2] [24] from the framed reference \(x_0\) to the designer's layout \(x_1\), with a stochastic-interpolant noise term [25] (\(\eta=1\)):

$$\begin{gathered}x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,x_1+\eta\sin^2(\pi t)\,\varepsilon,\qquad u_t=x_1-x_0+\eta\,\pi\sin(2\pi t)\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\\ \mathcal L_\text{FM}=\mathbb E_{t,(A,B),\varepsilon}\Big[\big\lVert v_\vartheta(x_t,t\mid A,B)-u_t\big\rVert^2_{\text{exist, category}}+\big\lVert v_\vartheta(x_t,t\mid A,B)-u_t\big\rVert^2_{\text{box}}\Big],\end{gathered}\tag{4}$$

the existence-and-category part and the box part averaged separately. In training, the source is the framed reference perturbed by one random scale per scene (positions \(\sigma_p\sim|\mathcal N(0,0.15)|\), yaw \(\sigma_\theta\sim|\mathcal N(0,\pi/4)|\)), so the flow transports a distribution of displaced references to the designers' layouts rather than regressing from one point; at inference it starts from the framed reference itself and integrates 100 Euler steps over \(t\in[0,1]\). Existence and category are decided by the plan (§3.3) and pinned on their straight path. An added piece is generated in the frame of its anchor, and its source is not Gaussian noise but a pose drawn from the data: the size and relative pose of a random training piece of its category next to an anchor of the anchor's category (79,939 poses over 816 category pairs, training split only). As the reference pieces start from the reference, an added piece thus starts from how designers place such a piece next to such an anchor.

3.3. Spatial adaptation: the edit plan and the division of labour

Plan heads. When the target differs from the reference, pieces must be given up, changed or added, and the discrete edit is decided before the continuous placement. Read on the framed reference, the state head gives \(p(\sigma)\) over \(\sigma\in\{\text{delete},\text{keep},\text{add}\}\) from the pooled slot and room tokens; the hotspot heads give two distributions \(h^+, h^-\) over a 16×16 grid on the target's bounding box — every cell a query attending to all tokens — of where designers add pieces and where the reference pieces they give up stand; the next-action head gives one distribution over the next edit of the current set: drop piece \(i\), change piece \(i\) to category \(c\), add a piece of category \(c\) (an anchor head picks the reference piece it goes next to), or stop. After every edit the slots are rewritten and the head is read again on what is kept.

Supervision of the discrete decisions. A designer's edits between the two rooms of a pair come without a recorded order, so a training row is a state of the pair: a random part of the designer's edits already applied to \(A\). With \(n_A, n_B\) the piece counts of a pair, \(q^\pm_g\) the shares of the designer's added and given-up pieces in cell \(g\), \(S_k\) a random intermediate state between \(A\)'s set and \(B\)'s (each edit already applied with probability \(u\sim U(0,1)\)), \(\mathcal A(S_k)\) the edits allowed there and \(\mathcal R(S_k)\) the designer's edits still to do,

$$\begin{gathered}\mathcal L_\text{state}=-\log p\big(\sigma^\ast\big),\qquad \sigma^\ast=\operatorname{sign}(n_B-n_A)+1\in\{\text{delete},\text{keep},\text{add}\},\\ \mathcal L_\text{hot}=-\sum_{g}\big(q^+_g\log h^+_g+q^-_g\log h^-_g\big),\\ \mathcal L_\text{act}=\log\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal A(S_k)}e^{\ell(e)}-\log\!\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal R(S_k)\cup\{\text{stop}\,:\,\mathcal R=\emptyset\}}\!\!e^{\ell(e)}\;-\;\log p\big(\text{anchor}^\ast\mid c\big),\end{gathered}\tag{5}$$

\(\mathcal L_\text{act}\) is the likelihood that the next edit is one of the designer's remaining edits, whichever their order, and that the head stops when none is left, so it learns which edit comes next from any intermediate layout rather than only the end state. A change of category is learned only within a 3D-FUTURE super-category (bed, sofa, chair, table, cabinet/shelf, …); a match across super-categories (a sofa where the designer put a bed) is learned as a drop and an addition. No annotation enters the labels.

Plans and their search. The state constrains the edits: delete allows drops and category changes (at least one drop), keep allows neither drops nor additions, add allows only additions (at least one). A category change is allowed only in the delete state, where the room loses furniture, so a larger room never trades a double bed for a single one. A drop's logit is raised by the delete hotspot at the piece's framed position, and an addition's score includes the add hotspot at its final cell. A plan \(\pi\) — a state and its edit sequence — therefore has the log-probability of Eq. 6, and each of the \(K=16\) candidates \(\Pi_K\) is placed by the flow and projected; we keep

$$\begin{gathered}\log p(\pi)=\log p(\sigma)+\sum_{k}\log p\big(e_k\mid e_{\lt k},\sigma,A,B\big)+\sum_{j\in\text{added}}\log h^+\big(g(p_j)\big),\\ \ell(\text{drop}\ i)\mathrel{+}=\log h^-\big(g(\tilde p_i)\big),\\ \pi^\ast=\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_K}\big(\,[\,\mathrm{viol}(\Pi_\theta(x_1^\pi))\le 2\,\text{cm}\,],\ \log p(\pi),\ -\overline{\lVert\Pi_\theta(x_1^\pi)-x_1^\pi\rVert}\,\big)\quad\text{(lexicographic)}\end{gathered}\tag{6}$$

the most probable plan whose projection leaves at most 2 cm of penetration or outside, and among equally probable ones (the same plan placed from other added-piece sources) the one \(\Pi_\theta\) moved least. The candidates are the most probable plan placed four times (one without category changes), the plans of the other two states, and the most probable plan carried on by 1, 2, … further edits of the head's own ranking (stop not allowed before), alternately with and without category changes. A room too small for the most probable plan thus receives the model's own next deletions until physics can fit it; at most 8 pieces are added.

Policy and constrained decoding. Read this way, the next-action head is a policy over the designer's edits, and the plan search is a constrained decoding around it, as in constrained decoding of sequence models: physics bounds how far the edits must go, the policy decides which edit comes next. There is no density budget, no count of pieces to give up and no ordering rule. The plan heads carry the decisions: adding the state head and the hotspots raises the share of feasible outputs on the validation split from 0.943 to 0.979 and lowers the colliding pairs at 0.7× from 0.328 to 0.233; letting the search carry the most probable plan on by the head's own next edits makes every output feasible (1.000; Table 11).

The division of labour. Which decisions should be learned and which left to deterministic rules and optimisation? Physical limits pushed into a network are met only up to its error, which shows as interpenetration and pieces crossing walls; a rule-based optimiser of the relation distance, conversely, has no notion of which pieces a designer gives up or adds (§5.2). IntentFlow learns what resists hand-coding — the room's state, where designers delete and add, which piece goes or comes next and when to stop, the continuous transport \(v_\vartheta\), whether a piece keeps its asset and which appearance it takes — and leaves to the projection what has an explicit physical definition: non-penetration, containment, door clearance and reachability, together with what the reference itself prescribes, its touching groups, wall contacts and angles. Every tolerance of these rules is measured in real rooms or taken from the definition of the metric that judges it. 3.4. Reference-prescribed feasibility projection \(\Pi_\theta\)

A flow meets non-convex boundary conditions only up to its error, which leaves small overlaps and pieces through walls. \(\Pi_\theta\) returns, per sample, the feasible layout closest to the flow's endpoint \(\hat x\):

$$\begin{aligned}\Pi_\theta(\hat x)=\arg\min_{p}\ &\sum_i\lVert p_i-\hat p_i\rVert^2\\ \text{s.t.}\ &\mathrm{pen}\big(M_i^h,M_j^h\big)\le0\ \ \forall h,\qquad \mathrm{pen}\big(F_i,F_j\big)\le\tau_{c_ic_j},\qquad \text{corners}(F_i)\subset P_B,\\ &\mathrm{pen}(F_i,b)\le0\ \ (b\in\mathcal D\cup\mathcal Q,\ z_i<1\,\text{m}),\end{aligned}\tag{7}$$

Penetration and allowances. \(M_i^h\) is the convex cross-section of piece \(i\)'s mesh at height \(h\) (17 heights, 0.05–2.45 m), so a chair may slide under a table top but not through its legs; \(F_i\) is its footprint. Instead of zero footprint overlap for every pair, which designers' rooms contradict, the allowance \(\tau_{c_ic_j}\) is the 90th percentile of the footprint penetration of the two categories in the training rooms (91 category pairs above 1 cm, e.g. a dining chair under its table; zero for two beds). \(\mathcal D\) are the door clearance boxes of the door-blocked metric, and \(\mathcal Q\) the reachability squares, added in up to four rounds along the cheapest paths that join every door and every free region to the largest free region of PhyScene's walkable raster (pieces below 1.5 m; the most walkable feasible round is kept). Feasibility is judged by penetration and containment only; door boxes and path squares are pursued by the same optimisation but do not decide it, since a local optimum between two door boxes would otherwise leave only the empty room feasible.

What the reference prescribes. In a reshaped room not all reference relations can be kept, and the flow's endpoint carries its error into every group. Before the solve, three steps restore what the reference itself fixes, and the solve then removes any overlap they create. Orientation: a kept piece takes its own reference angle to the wall it stands against (folded; a reference angle within 6° of square counts as square, the emptiest 1° bin of the training rooms' deviations); an added piece is turned to square or 45° when that is the maximum-a-posteriori angle under its category's deviation prior and the flow's measured orientation noise, and keeps its generated angle otherwise. "Square" is to the wall the piece touches or to the room's dominant direction, whichever is the smaller turn, so a piece meeting a cut corner with one corner is not turned onto the slanted wall. Groups: pieces touching in the reference (gap ≤ 5 cm) form a group; every kept member returns to its reference offset and relative angle to the group's largest piece, which stays where the flow put it. Wall contact: a group whose member stood against a wall is moved rigidly towards that wall until the member is within its category's 90th-percentile real wall gap. Walls and groups compete: holding every reference wall contact piece by piece raises the wall contacts kept on the validation split from 0.664 to 0.985 but tears 0.247 of the touching pairs, against 0.177 (Table 11, row K), so whole groups are moved instead.

Solver. With the constraints written \(g_k(p)\le0\), the augmented Lagrangian [3] minimises

$$\mathcal L_\rho(p,\mu)=\sum_i\lVert p_i-\hat p_i\rVert^2+\sum_k\mu_k\,g_k(p)_++\frac\rho2\sum_k g_k(p)_+^2,\qquad \mu_k\leftarrow\mu_k+\rho\,g_k(p)_+,\quad \rho\leftarrow2\rho,$$

with 100 Adam [27] steps (learning rate 0.02) per outer round, \(\rho_0=10\), at most 25 rounds, stopping below 1 cm. Assets are retrieved before the solve (§3.5), and the projection works on their meshes. The parts only translate, so every separating axis and every part's extent along it is fixed: the overlap of two parts along an axis is a constant plus the projection of their offset, and a step costs a few small tensor operations. The 16 candidates of a target are solved together as one batch on the GPU, each with its own multipliers, penalty and stopping, and the inner Adam steps are captured as a CUDA graph (§5.9). The hard constraints are not learned: on the endpoint estimate \(\hat x_1=\mathbb E[x_1\mid x_t]\) a non-convex feasible set can make them infeasible even for the exact flow (the mean of a chair left and right of a doorway lies in the doorway), and on samples any position error violates constraints on whose boundary the training layouts lie, so the projection enforces them per sample.

3.5. Layout-appearance separation by a style head

People who move often want new furniture in another style while keeping the arrangement they are used to. Because IntentFlow carries the arrangement in the poses the flow produces and in what the projection restores, not in particular 3D assets, the appearance of the furniture can change while the layout is kept.

Assets and style. A same-asset head decides whether a kept piece keeps its own 3D-FUTURE asset; otherwise a style head predicts an appearance vector \(a_i\in\mathbb R^{32}\) and the asset is retrieved by appearance and size:

$$a_i=a_i^{\text{own}}+\lambda\,\big(a_i^{\text{style}}-a_i^{\text{own}}\big),\qquad k_i=\arg\min_{k\in\mathrm{cat}(c_i)}\frac{\lVert E_k-a_i\rVert^2}{n_a}+\frac{\lVert\log \bar s_k-\log s_i\rVert^2}{n_s},\tag{8}$$

where \(a_i^{\text{style}}\) is the style head's prediction given the target style label, \(a_i^{\text{own}}\) its prediction given the reference's own, \(E_k\) and \(\bar s_k\) the catalogue asset's appearance vector and size, and \(n_a, n_s\) normalise the two terms by their mean over random asset pairs. A substituted or added piece never takes an asset whose footprint exceeds the predicted one (beyond the 2 cm tolerance) while a fitting one exists.

Appearance vectors. Each catalogue asset \(k\) has an appearance vector \(z_k\) (\(E_k\) in Eq. 8), an MLP of its room co-membership embedding (a PPMI factorisation, cf. [28]), its CLIP image features [29] and its 3D-FUTURE style, theme and material labels, trained to rank the designer's asset of a held-out piece against same-category assets by the mean appearance of the room's other pieces [30], with a supervised contrastive term on the style label [31]:

$$\mathcal L_z=-\log\frac{\exp(\langle z_{k^\ast},\bar z_{\text{room}\setminus k^\ast}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{k\in\mathrm{cat}(k^\ast)}\exp(\langle z_k,\bar z_{\text{room}\setminus k^\ast}\rangle/\gamma)}+\mu\,\mathcal L_\text{SupCon}(z;\text{style label}),\tag{9}$$

with \(\gamma=0.1\).

Why the layout is kept. The style label is an input of the style and same-asset heads only, never of the trunk: the flow sees a piece's category and bounding-box size, never its visual appearance, and the groups the projection restores are formulated on footprints. Asset retrieval selects target-style models of the piece's category near its predicted size; because the flow and \(\Pi_\theta\) accommodate the bounded footprint variations, the spatial configuration does not depend on particular 3D assets. On the held-out test split, switching the appearance across three styles results in a relational drift of only \(D\in[0.027, 0.034]\) from the reference (§5.6): the appearance follows the target style while the arrangement stays anchored to the reference.

4. Data and Training

Corpus. We use 3D-FRONT [1] rooms with 3 to 24 pieces after removing ceiling and pendant lamps, none more than 20% outside the room, and the 3D-FUTURE assets [12], split by house into training, validation and test (80 / 10 / 10%). No model sees validation or test rooms in training, and training rooms whose design reappears in the validation or test split are removed.

Real designer pairs. The training set has 68,958 pairs (293 validation), in four kinds. Partner pairs (6,480) are rooms of the same type from different homes whose category-family sets have Jaccard similarity above 0.6 and whose area ratio lies in [0.5, 2]: two designers' arrangements of similar furniture. Downsizing pairs (23,965) are two rooms of the same type furnished by designers where the smaller holds part of the larger room's furniture, and upsizing pairs (21,289) are the same pairs reversed: what a designer gave up, changed or added between a room and a smaller or larger one. Self pairs (17,224) are a room and its own layout with the axes rescaled by 0.95–1.05. Pieces are matched within families by footprint size [32]. Every decision has a target in these pairs — the state from the piece counts, the hotspots from the given-up and added pieces, the next actions from the designer's edits — and no annotation enters the labels. The placement loss is applied to pairs whose arrangement the designer kept (\(D<0.15\)) and to the added pieces of every pair; half of every batch comes from such pairs, and rows are reweighted to balance the area ratios (bins at 0.7, 0.9, 1.1, 1.44, 2.0).

Masked rooms. Real pairs rarely tell where a missing piece belongs in this very room. With probability 0.7 a self pair becomes a completion row: 1 to 30% of its pieces (at most 8) are removed from \(A\) and become \(B\)'s added pieces at their own poses, so the model learns which piece a room lacks and where it belongs [23].

Label fixes. The corpus preprocessing had merged two 3D-FUTURE fine categories into other classes: footstools (“Footstool / Sofastool / Bed End Stool / Stool”) were labelled double bed, and bookcases wardrobe. We relabel them stool and bookcase. Category changes and asset retrieval stay within these labels.

Training. All heads and the flow share the trunk and are trained jointly (losses summed with weight 1) with AdamW [33] (weight decay 0.01), a one-cycle schedule [34], gradient clipping at 1, batch 128 per GPU, bf16 autocast, on 2 x NVIDIA L4 (DDP). The model was trained in stages, each initialised from the previous one and adding one component (Appendix A, Table 14); the last stage runs 12,000 steps. The checkpoint is chosen by validation flow loss.

5. Experiments

5.1. Setup

Test sets. All comparisons of §5.2–§5.6 use a test set frozen once from the held-out test rooms with fixed selection rules and never used for training: 700 standard-test targets — 50 reference rooms per test (64 distinct) with 6–12 pieces including a bed, a sofa with a TV or a dining set, each scaled by 0.7×, 0.8×, 0.9×, 1.0×, 1.1×, 1.2×, 1.3× (scale test) and reshaped into seven outlines no smaller than the reference (shape test: 1.2×1.3, cut, L, T, long, pentagon, trapezoid) — 285 real designer pairs \((A,B)\), in which a method furnishes \(B\)'s empty room from \(A\), 200 completion rooms and 50 style rooms. The set is stored with a sha256 manifest (Appendix D); each baseline was run once on it and every method's layouts are re-scored by the same code. The ablations (§5.7) and the qualitative views of Appendix E use the validation split, whose standard tests have 10 rooms each; §5.8 repeats the main comparison there.

Metrics. Kept: share of reference pieces in the output. Rel. kept: of the reference's floor pairs within 0.3 m, the share still within 0.3 m with the relative yaw within 20° and the side within 45°. \(D\) (Eq. 2, over the kept pieces) measures how well the reference arrangement is kept and is read together with kept. iRecall: share of the reference's in-front-of / beside / facing / aligned / against-wall triplets realised. Wall kept: share of the reference's wall contacts (≤ 0.15 m) kept. Square: floor pieces within 5° of square to their nearest wall. Torn: of the floor pairs touching in the reference (≤ 5 cm), the share more than 30 cm apart. Out: floor pieces more than 5% outside the room. Collide: floor pairs overlapping by more than 2% of the smaller footprint, nestable pairs included (a chair under its table counts), so it is not a count of violations of \(\Pi_\theta\)'s constraints. Door blocked: door boxes more than 5% covered.

Realism without \(D\). \(D\) is our own measure, and the energy reference minimises a form of it. As measures that depend on neither, we follow PhyScene [6]: every layout is rendered top-down (256 px, 6 cm per pixel, category colours, the same for every method), and FID [35] and KID [36] (clean-fid [37]) are computed against the real rooms of the test split; SCA is the accuracy of a classifier trained to tell real from generated renders (0.5 is indistinguishable); with them, the share of colliding boxes, the share of layouts with a piece outside, and the walkable / reachable shares of PhyScene's raster. On real pairs the reference is designer \(B\)'s own room; CKL is the KL divergence of the category distributions.

Baselines. All use released checkpoints. LEGO-Net [4] receives every reference piece its vocabulary covers at its affine transplant pose in the target, with the target floor plan, and denoises it with the authors' settings; other pieces stay at the transplant; it keeps everything and adds nothing. PhyScene [6] receives only the target room — its released model has no object-list or arrangement conditioning — and samples with its collision, room-layout and reachability guidance. MiDiffusion [5] receives the target floor plan and the reference's object labels (91.9% of the pieces have a class it knows). The generated objects of PhyScene and MiDiffusion are matched to the reference's pieces by category, and \(D\) is computed over the matched pieces. MiDiffusion was trained on 20 of the 64 reference rooms of the standard tests (its released split), which favours it; its released weights turn 39.2% of its wall-side pieces to face the wall (the references: 3.5%), which works against it. Two references take more of the reference and are listed in grey. The affine transplant maps every reference piece between the two rooms' minimum rotated rectangles; it is LEGO-Net's input and keeps every piece. Transplant + energy optimisation (in the spirit of [10] [11]) needs no learning: the transplant's positions are optimised for a smooth form of the per-relation distance of Eq. 1 over all pairs, with the reference's wall sides kept against walls, the room and the door boxes as constraints; every piece is kept and nothing is added. It minimises a form of the measure \(D\) reports, so \(D\) favours it by construction; its solve leaves more than 1 cm of constraint violation on 17.0% of the standard-test targets.

Statistics. Differences are mean paired differences over the same targets, with 95% intervals and two-sided \(p\) from a bootstrap [38] that resamples reference rooms (all targets of a room together; 10,000 resamples). Appendix B lists every test.

Physical and architectural feasibility. IntentFlow leaves 0.2% of its pieces outside the room (LEGO-Net 13.0%, MiDiffusion 11.5%, PhyScene 10.8%), blocks 0.010 of the doors, and 0.070 of its floor pairs collide (all baselines higher, all p<0.001). Stronger references. 6. Discussion, limitations and conclusion

6.1. Limitations and failure modes

The plan decides what goes, and physics decides only how far the plan must go; but the decisions are not always the designer's. A room can lose a piece a designer would keep, an enlarged room can lose small pieces such as plants (Fig. 4), and at 0.7× the colliding share rises to 0.118 (the measure also counts pieces nested at different heights). The keep / drop reading of the heads is not calibrated (ECE 0.170); the decisions rest on the next-action head's ranking and the search, not on this probability. Additions remain the weakest part: the right category is added in 0.33 of the completion rooms, a correct addition lands 0.82 m (mean) from where the designer had it, and a piece may still go to the wrong wall; the state head and hotspots, which make the plans feasible, lowered the completion rate on the validation split (Table 11, E). A pair the flow pulls apart is restored by the projection only if it touched in the reference. In reshaped rooms, keeping wall sides and keeping groups conflict (§5.7); moving whole groups keeps the groups, at the price of fewer wall contacts than a per-piece wall constraint would keep.

On real designer pairs MiDiffusion, which generates its own arrangement, is closer to the designers' rooms by FID, KID and SCA, and MiDiffusion and PhyScene are more walkable (Table 7). IntentFlow keeps the reference's arrangement, which the target's designer did not; by these measures that choice costs realism. On the scale test alone the affine transplant keeps more relations than IntentFlow, at the price of pieces outside the room. The baselines are run with their released checkpoints: LEGO-Net and PhyScene are living-room checkpoints run on every room type, PhyScene cannot receive the reference, LEGO-Net sees only pieces in its vocabulary, and MiDiffusion was trained on 20 of the 64 standard-test reference rooms; the energy reference optimises a form of \(D\).

Some choices were made on the test split. The group terms of \(\Pi_\theta\) and the orientation rule's smaller turn were chosen after inspecting test-split figures; the earlier rule (square to any touched wall) and the final one differ by 0.025 in shape-test relations kept, and the style test ran with the earlier rule. Physics is a rule, and so are the reference-prescribed steps of \(\Pi_\theta\) (groups, walls, angles), although their tolerances are measured in real rooms. The projection covers doors and reachability; window fronts and the clearance needed to pull out a chair or open a wardrobe are not constraints of this version. Decisions of the search depend on whether \(\Pi_\theta\)'s residual is below 2 cm, so another floating-point order (another device or solver implementation) can change individual targets. A target takes 6.5 s (median), but the slowest take 15.5 s, mostly in the reachability rounds.

Three further limits follow from the design. Strongly non-convex rooms. The flow starts from one similarity map of the reference into the target, about the two centroids; in deep L-shaped rooms or open plans with several zones a single global map can carry a group into a zone where it does not belong, and the flow, which moves pieces locally, does not reassign zones; a partition of the room or several starts would be needed. Category labels. Mixed labels remain in the corpus, and a change of category stays within a 3D-FUTURE super-category, so a designer's swap across super-categories is learned only as a drop and an addition. One hypothesis. The search returns the most probable feasible plan; other equally plausible layouts are not offered, and returning several hypotheses would suit a design workflow better.

6.2. Broader impact

IntentFlow is meant to help people reuse an arrangement they like, and their existing furniture, in a new home without hiring a designer. It is trained on the public 3D-FRONT rooms and 3D-FUTURE assets and uses no personal data. The user keeps control of what the method cannot know: the reference, the target room, the style and the degree of change \(\lambda\); the method proposes a layout and does not replace a designer's judgement, in particular on safety and accessibility beyond the clearances it models.

6.3. Conclusion

We have presented IntentFlow for reference-to-room arrangement transfer, which learns the design decisions of the task from designers' room pairs and keeps physics as a projection. The reference, framed into the target, the walls and openings of both rooms and the layout being generated are tokens of one attention trunk; a state head, delete and add hotspots and a next-action head edit the furniture set, a rectified flow carries the edited reference to its place, and a style head changes the furniture's appearance with one degree of change while the arrangement drifts by \(D\le0.034\). A mesh-level augmented Lagrangian projection, with tolerances measured in real rooms, makes the layout feasible and restores the reference's groups, walls and angles, and the most probable feasible plan is kept. On a frozen held-out test split IntentFlow keeps the reference's relations (0.69) and arrangement (\(D=0.059\)) better than LEGO-Net, MiDiffusion and PhyScene, leaves 0.2% of its pieces outside the room, is more realistic and walkable than the three baselines by PhyScene's metrics on the transfer targets, and runs in 6.5 s (median) per target. Adding pieces where designers add them, matching designers' rooms when the target's designer chose another arrangement, and resolving the conflict between walls and groups in irregular rooms remain open.
